How to play

Literature — Canadian Fish, or just Fish — is questions, memory, and deduction. The move is simple: ask an opponent for one exact card. The game is everything you hear, remember, and infer from that.

The goal

You and two teammates collect complete sets — also called half-suits or books. Eight sets are in play. Capture more than the other team and you win.

You never win a set alone. Cards keep moving, so a set belongs to the team once all six sit on your side — in one hand or spread across partners. The trick is knowing when that’s true.

Unlike Go Fish, you only ask opponents, never teammates. Watch every question. Memory and inference are the game.

The eight sets

Strip the four 8s from a 52-card deck. Forty-eight left. Each suit splits in two: low 2–7 and high 9–A. The 8 is gone, so the halves never meet.

  • ♠ low — 2♠3♠4♠5♠6♠7♠
  • ♠ high — 9♠10♠J♠Q♠K♠A♠
  • ♥ low — 2♥3♥4♥5♥6♥7♥
  • ♥ high — 9♥10♥J♥Q♥K♥A♥
  • ♦ low — 2♦3♦4♦5♦6♦7♦
  • ♦ high — 9♦10♦J♦Q♦K♦A♦
  • ♣ low — 2♣3♣4♣5♣6♣7♣
  • ♣ high — 9♣10♣J♣Q♣K♣A♣

Each player is dealt 8 cards. Never show your hand — not even to partners.

Teams and seating

Two teams of three, sitting alternately: A1 — B1 — A2 — B2 — A3 — B3 (and B3 sits next to A1). Everyone sits between two opponents. The host can keep that split, randomize it, or move people; both sides stay three-and-three.

You cannot plan out loud or show cards. All communication is the questions you ask, and the ones you skip. Keep voice chat for banter, not strategy.

Your turn

Ask one opponent for one exact card. A question is legal only if all four of these are true:

  1. Be specific. “The 7 of Spades,” never “a spade.”
  2. Hold that set. A high-spade ask needs a high spade in your hand.
  3. Ask for a hole. You cannot name a card you already hold.
  4. Ask an opponent who still has cards — never a teammate.

Example

Your only spade is the Q♠. You may ask for the 9♠ 10♠ J♠ K♠ or A♠ — not the Queen, and not any low spade.

What happens next

  • Hit. They pass it face-up. You add it to your hand and ask again — anyone, any legal card — until you miss.
  • Miss. The turn becomes theirs. The table still learned that you hold that set, just not that card, and that they didn’t have it.

Claiming a set

When your team has all six, claim on your turn. Hold them all: lay down. Spread across partners: assign who has which. Then one of three things happens:

  • Correct. Your team keeps the set. Those cards leave play.
  • Wrong split. You had them all, but named the wrong hands. The set is dead. Nobody scores.
  • They held one. The set goes to the other team.

Everyone sees the result. The six cards are shown, then stacked with the team that scored — or thrown in if the set died. That’s public, like the score. (You still may not write anything down.)

Example: you hold 2, 3, 4, 6, 7♥. Doug earlier asked for 3♥, so he holds a low heart — the 5. You say: “Claim low hearts — Doug has the 5, I have the rest.” Correct: you score.

A claim is a commitment: name the full layout. You keep the turn after. If it emptied your hand, pass to a teammate with cards.

Ending and winning

The game ends when all 8 sets are claimed or forfeited. Most sets wins. Five or more is a lock. 4–4 is a tie. A dead set belongs to no one, so 4–3 happens.

Empty hands can’t be asked. Claim yourself empty: choose a teammate with cards. When a whole team is out, the other team must claim what’s left — no more questions. Track who is empty as hard as who holds what.

Reading the table

  • Anyone may ask what the last question and answer were. Older history stays in your head.
  • Anyone may ask how many cards a player holds. Answer truthfully.
  • Claimed sets are public: which team scored them, and which were forfeited.
  • No notes, apps, or counters. Brain only.

“A asked for 9♥” means A holds a high heart, not the 9. If it hits B, A now has the 9 and B is lighter. Stack those facts.

Strategy

From game one

  • Track every question. Who, what, yes or no.
  • Drop dead sets. If you can’t ask in it, forget it.
  • Don’t claim on a hunch. Sure, then prompt.

Next

  • Safe sets can’t be stolen. Lock other sets first.
  • Ask to inform. A miss can tell partners you hold that set.
  • Exhaust one rank across the table, then chase a void.

What veterans still miss

  • Starve a dangerous opponent of turns. A miss hands them the ask.
  • Skip the tug-of-war. Trading one card leaks the whole table.
  • Partner signal: they miss 2♥ → you hold it, ask a different low heart; you lack it, repeat 2♥; you’re void, switch sets.
